The Crimean (Kerch) Bridge is a 19-kilometre road and rail crossing that, according to Reuters, is the only direct land link between Russia's transport network and the Crimean peninsula. It has been hit repeatedly since 2022 and, after the early attacks, was repaired and reopened. After the strike of 17 November 2026, how long repairs will take is unclear.

This is an explainer, not a report on the latest attack: how the bridge was built, why it is considered tough, and what each attack did and how long it took to fix. For the first report on the latest strike, see Drone hits Kerch Bridge arch as Russia claims record 1,336 intercepts.

What kind of structure is it?

  • Length: about 19 km in total (Reuters). Taken separately, the road bridge is roughly 17 km and the rail bridge roughly 18 km.
  • Two bridges, not one: two parallel structures, a four-lane road bridge and a double-track rail bridge, each independent of the other.
  • Main span: a steel arch carries both over the shipping channel in the Kerch Strait. The longest span is 227 metres, with 35 metres of clearance beneath.
  • Foundations: roughly 7,000 piles were driven deep into the seabed.
  • Cost: about 227.9 billion roubles according to the project's customer, which TASS reported as roughly $3.5 billion.
  • Builder: Stroygazmontazh, owned by Arkady Rotenberg. Main construction began in 2016.

When did it open?

Vladimir Putin opened the road bridge on 15 May 2018 by driving a Kamaz truck across it (RFE/RL). The rail bridge entered service in December 2019, with freight trains following in mid-2020. Sources vary by days or months on exact opening dates, so rely on the years.

Why is it considered tough?

Three reasons. First, the design: two independent crossings mean one can sometimes carry traffic while the other is damaged, which is why traffic was never fully cut after the 2022 and 2023 attacks. Second, scale: deep piled foundations and heavy steel and concrete make total destruction by a single blast difficult. Third, layers of protection:

  • Reports citing UK Ministry of Defence assessments say Russia placed sunken and floating barges, nets, mines and smoke generators around the bridge against naval drones, and added air defences (Kyiv Post).
  • Satellite imagery shows the number of barges changing over time, so the barriers are not fixed (Kyiv Independent).

Tough, however, is not the same as untouchable. The timeline below shows a bridge that takes hits and, until now, usually comes back.

Timeline: when and how it was attacked

8 October 2022: truck bomb

An explosion on the road bridge dropped part of the road decking on the Crimea-bound side into the water and set fuel tank cars on a passing train on fire. Reports put the death toll between three and five. Russia's investigators said they had traced the truck's route and a network behind it. Kyiv did not claim the attack at the time; in July 2023 senior Ukrainian officials and the SBU acknowledged it. A single lane of light traffic reopened the same day.

17 July 2023: naval drones

Russia's National Anti-Terrorist Committee said two Ukrainian uncrewed surface vessels struck the bridge. Russian officials said two people were killed and their daughter was injured; the road span was damaged and rail services stopped (AP). Ukraine later acknowledged the attack.

3 June 2025: underwater charges

The SBU said it detonated about 1,100 kg of TNT equivalent against the bridge's underwater supports. Russia's bridge operator said traffic was halted for about three hours; the extent of damage could not be independently verified (Euronews).

16-17 November 2026: two waves

On the morning of 16 November a drone hit the bridge's arch and traffic was restricted. A second attack followed overnight into 17 November. The SBU said one metal arch and the road span beneath it were "completely destroyed" and the rail section also damaged; President Zelensky called it the seventh stage of an operation lasting several weeks. Footage geolocated by NBC News shows a partial collapse (NBC News). Moscow acknowledged structural damage without detailing its extent; Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the bridge would be restored, and Crimea's Russian-installed head Sergey Aksyonov said rail traffic had partly resumed. The weapons Ukraine named (Palianytsia, Neptune, Flamingo and others) come from Ukrainian and company statements and are not independently verified (The War Zone).

Analysts now paint a different picture: Emil Kastehelmi of Black Bird Group said the road bridge "won't be repaired during this war". That is an opinion, not an official estimate.

Strategic importance

Reuters describes the bridge as the only direct link between Russia's network and Crimea, used since the 2022 invasion to move troops, fuel and ammunition toward southern Ukraine, and vital for supplying the peninsula, including Sevastopol. For Putin it is a symbol of the 2014 annexation; for Ukraine, cutting it is an openly stated military objective.

There is a counterpoint. Sevastopol governor Mikhail Razvozhayev said the bridge was "practically not used" for cargo, and passenger ferries are running. Ukraine's General Staff claimed the Chonhar and Henichesk bridges also went down this autumn, which is unverified. The collapsed arch sits over the channel between the Sea of Azov and the Black Sea, so shipping could be affected too.
