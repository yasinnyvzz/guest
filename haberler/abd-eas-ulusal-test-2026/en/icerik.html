The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) conducted a nationwide test of the Emergency Alert System (EAS) on Tuesday, November 17, 2026. The test interrupted television, radio, cable and satellite programming for about one minute; phones did not receive an alert.

When and how the test ran

The test began at 2:20 p.m. Eastern (22:20 in Türkiye). Broadcasters interrupted regular programming to play a message of roughly one minute. According to officials beforehand, it read: "This is a nationwide test of the Emergency Alert System, issued by the Federal Emergency Management Agency. This is only a test. No action is required by the public." Some broadcasters could carry it in both English and Spanish, and alert tones accompanied the message for viewers and listeners who are deaf or hard of hearing. A photo of a TV screen shared on social media shows the message as a three-page on-screen text covering "2:20 PM NOV 17, 2026 to 2:35 PM"; the photo's origin has not been verified.

What differs from 2023: no phone alerts

The last nationwide test, in October 2023, also included Wireless Emergency Alerts, so phones sounded too. This time only radio and television were involved. In FEMA's words, "your cell phone will not beep, buzz or receive a notification."

Why it was held

Federal law requires a test of this kind at least once every three years. It lets officials find technical or operational problems before the system is needed in a real emergency. The EAS is a national public warning system made up of radio, TV, cable, satellite and wireline video providers, and through it the president can address the public during a national emergency. The same tones are used by local, state and tribal authorities for more localized alerts.

FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton said that "during an emergency, every second is crucial," and that the test would help identify and fix issues before a real event.

Answering the rumors

The timing sparked speculation online after several large outages hit banking, airline and cloud services in recent days. FEMA said the test was routine and unrelated to those disruptions, adding that there was no secret emergency. It had also noted that the test could be postponed if a real emergency or significant weather event occurred.
