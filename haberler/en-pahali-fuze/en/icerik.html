There is no single "most expensive missile in the world." The answer depends on what you count: the price paid for one missile, the program cost divided by every missile built, or an interceptor versus a strike weapon. Using published US budget figures, the highest numbers belong to missile-defense interceptors and to the Sentinel ICBM, while the weapons fired most often cost a few million dollars each.

First, which "cost" are we talking about?

Budget documents use several measures, and they are not interchangeable:

  • Flyaway (or unit procurement) cost covers the missile itself, as bought in a given year.
  • Weapon-system or program unit cost adds support equipment, and in some cases development, spread across the missiles built.
  • Total program cost is the whole effort, which says little about the price of one round.

Figures below come from different years and dollar bases, so treat the ranking as indicative, not exact.

The Sentinel: the biggest price tag per missile in the offensive force

The Air Force told Congress in January 2024 that Sentinel had breached the Nunn-McCurdy cost limits, with the unit cost rising by at least 37 percent from a $118 million baseline to about $162 million in 2020 dollars, according to Breaking Defense. The Congressional Research Service notes that the Pentagon completed its review in July 2024 and allowed the program to continue, and that the plan covers 634 production missiles plus 25 for testing, with 400 to be deployed (CRS, Defense Primer: LGM-35A Sentinel). Reporting on that decision put the restructured program at about $140.9 billion, 81 percent above the September 2020 estimate (Air & Space Forces Magazine). The program figure is not a price per missile; it includes silos, command and launch facilities and development. For the missile's warhead shield, see our report on the Mk21A flight test.

Interceptors: the costliest rounds, and the ones that run out first

The Pentagon's cost office estimated in 2021 that the Next Generation Interceptor would average $498 million each for 31 missiles when development is included, and $111 million each to buy the 21 operational ones; the Arms Control Association put purchase cost for the newest legacy Ground-Based Interceptors at $90 to $100 million per missile (Arms Control Association, June 2021). These are older estimates. The Missile Defense Agency wanted to field NGI by 2028, and a June 2024 GAO review found the plans risky (Defense News).

The ship-based SM-3 Block IIA is priced at $28.7 million per round in Pentagon budget documents, according to The War Zone, a figure that excludes development, infrastructure and sustainment. The FY2027 request seeks 136 of them, against 12 in FY2024 (Naval News, April 2026; HigherGov budget data).

For THAAD, the Missile Defense Agency's FY2024 justification book listed a flyaway unit cost of $12.336 million and a gross weapon-system unit cost of $19.707 million for 11 interceptors. A December 2025 CSIS brief cites $15 million as the FY2026 estimated unit cost (CSIS). The same document shows why the label matters: the same missile carries two very different prices depending on the measure. Production plans are covered in our report on Lockheed Martin's PAC-3 and THAAD expansion.

Strike missiles: hypersonic and ballistic

The Army's Dark Eagle long-range hypersonic missile has no confirmed public price. CRS cites a January 2023 CBO estimate of $41 million per missile (2023 dollars) for 300 similar weapons, and says Army officials indicated the flyaway cost of the eight missiles in the FY2025 request would exceed that, while future costs could fall as quantities rise (CRS, LRHW: Dark Eagle).

The Navy's fact file lists the Trident II D5 at $30.9 million per missile, with a 1990 deployment date. The page is undated and the number has not been adjusted for inflation or for later life-extension work, so it is a floor, not a current price.

Cruise missiles and the cheap end

The Tomahawk Block V appears at $1.89 million per round in budget data compiled by The War Zone. The FY2027 Navy request is for 785 Tomahawks at about $3.009 billion (Global Defense Corp, April 2026); dividing gives roughly $3.8 million, our own arithmetic, and the budget line may include costs beyond the missile. The Air Force's FY2026 request priced the JASSM at about $2.6 million and the LRASM at about $3.6 million (Air & Space Forces Magazine). For background, see our cruise missile explainer, the report on Tomahawk warhead production and the LRASM sensor deliveries.

The PAC-3 MSE interceptor now costs about $5.3 million in the Army's FY2027 request, up from a historical average of around $4 million (The War Zone, May 2026). Our report on RTX's production ramp-up covers the SM-6 and SM-3 IB side.

What is missing: Russian and Chinese missiles

Systems such as Russia's Kh-47M2 Kinzhal and Sarmat, or China's DF-series missiles, are sometimes named as the "most expensive." We found no credible published unit price for any of them, and no government budget document of the kind used above. Any number quoted for them is an estimate without a verifiable basis.

So which is the most expensive?

  • By average program cost per missile: the NGI estimate ($498 million) and Sentinel (about $162 million in 2020 dollars) lead the published figures.
  • By price of a production missile: homeland interceptors (NGI at $111 million, legacy GBI at $90-100 million) sit at the top, then the Dark Eagle (CBO's $41 million), Trident II D5 ($30.9 million) and SM-3 Block IIA ($28.7 million).
  • By what gets fired in volume: Tomahawk, JASSM, PAC-3 MSE and SM-6 are priced in the low millions, and their budgets are growing quickly.

The most expensive missile is therefore the answer to a narrower question than the headline asks, and the published numbers behind it come with large caveats about year, scope and estimating basis.
