M27 IAR Explained: The Marine Corps' HK416-Based Infantry Rifle

The M27 Infantry Automatic Rifle (IAR) is the U.S. Marine Corps' standard infantry rifle: a select-fire 5.56×45mm NATO weapon made by Heckler & Koch and based on the HK416, with a 16.5-inch barrel, a short-stroke gas piston and a 30-round magazine. The Corps chose it in 2010 to take over the automatic-rifleman job from the belt-fed M249. From 2018 it went to riflemen across infantry squads. In February 2026 the Corps said it would keep the M27 and would not adopt the Army's 6.8mm M7.

On 6 November 2026 the Corps raised the ceiling of its sole-source M27 contract with H&K to about $84.6 million and extended ordering to April 2028; the details are in our report on the M27 contract increase. This page explains the rifle itself.

What the M27 is, and how close it is to the HK416

Mechanically, the M27 is an HK416. A short-stroke piston drives an operating rod against the bolt carrier, instead of venting gas into the receiver as the direct-impingement M16 and M4 do. It fires from a closed bolt. In 2011 Military Times called it "a 16.5″ HK416D" with Marine-specified accessories: no heavier barrel, identical internals.

H&K's programme manager at the time, Robbie Reidsma, told Small Arms Defense Journal: "it's the HK416 system." The gas-block exhaust port was enlarged slightly (the Marine Corps said hot-weather testing favoured a slower cyclic rate), and then-new H&K features such as a captive firing-pin retaining pin and a reversible charging handle were built in. "M27" is a Marine Corps designation: Reidsma said H&K could sell others an HK416 in the same configuration, but not an M27.

From SAW substitute to standard rifle: programme history

The Infantry Automatic Rifle requirement (2000–2013)

Work on a lighter, more accurate alternative to the 23-pound M249 began around 2000. After an open competition, H&K received an indefinite-delivery contract in 2008, and in 2010 its rifle was designated M27. According to a 2017 GAO decision, the Corps bought 24 IARs in 2009, 458 in 2010 and 6,042 in 2011, which used up that contract's maximum quantity. The rifle's first combat use came in April 2011, when 1st Battalion, 3rd Marines took it to Garmser, Afghanistan. In June 2011 the Commandant approved fielding.

The M27 was meant to work alongside the SAW: every automatic rifleman in infantry fire teams and LAR scout teams got one, while rifle companies kept about six M249s. By 2017 most rifle companies held 27 IARs and six SAWs.

The 2017–2018 decisions

In early 2017 the Commandant, Gen. Robert Neller, said he wanted an M27 for every 0311 rifleman. That August the Corps announced plans to buy 11,000 from H&K to replace M4s in infantry squads (all but the squad leader's), with a notice allowing up to 50,814 rifles (Marine Corps Times printed 50,184). FN America protested; on 12 December 2017 the GAO denied the protest, accepting that switching vendors would duplicate substantial costs and delay fielding by four to six years.

In April 2018 H&K received a sole-source contract for up to 15,000 M27 systems ($29.4 million ceiling); the first 5,660 or so cost about $1,300 each. By April 2019 the Corps had bought just over 14,000 and planned to arm every infantryman from platoon commander down by mid-2021. Marine Raiders kept the M4 and M249.

Upgrades: suppressors, SCO and the Squad Aiming Laser

Suppressors. In December 2020 Marine Corps Systems Command began mass fielding of suppressors for the M27, M4 and M4A1 to infantry, reconnaissance and special operations units, aiming for about 30,000 by fiscal 2023. Its infantry weapons officer said the main gain was communication within and between squads in a firefight.

Squad Common Optic. A February 2020 Trijicon contract worth up to $64 million covers about 19,000 1–8x SCOs, replacing the fixed 4x Rifle Combat Optic; fielding began in January 2021.

Squad Aiming Laser. On 27–28 July 2026 PAE Marine Corps began fielding the Squad Aiming Laser at School of Infantry-East, Camp Lejeune. Replacing the PEQ-16, it is about three ounces lighter and nearly half an inch shorter, easing the M27's front-end weight. Envision Technology holds a $249 million contract (February 2025) to supply it through 2030. Rifles have new jobs too: Marines now train to shoot drones with them.

Why the Marines are not switching to the Army's M7

The Army is replacing the M4 and M249 with SIG Sauer's 6.8mm M7 rifle and M250 machine gun, starting with the 25th Infantry Division in Hawaii. On 13 February 2026 Task & Purpose reported that the Marines would not follow. Lt. Col. Eric Flanagan of Marine Corps Combat Development Command said the M27 "best aligns with our unique service requirements, amphibious doctrinal employment of weapons and distinct modernization priorities", ensures "seamless interoperability across the Joint force and with coalition partners", and that the Corps would "continue to monitor development of the M7".

Ammunition is part of that argument: the M7 carries 20 rounds per magazine, while the M27 keeps 30-round magazines and the 5.56mm round common to allies (see 5.56 NATO vs 7.62 NATO compared). In May 2020 Marine Corps Systems Command had already corrected a release that wrongly said the M27 would be replaced by the Next Generation Squad Weapon.

Criticisms

  • Cost, sole-sourcing and foreign production. At a March 2018 House Armed Services Committee hearing, lawmakers cited about $3,000 per M27 against $642 for an M4; Rep. Joe Wilson questioned not competing a buy of up to 50,814 rifles, and Rep. Austin Scott said U.S. firms could build an equally good rifle. The committee moved to withhold 20% of M27 funding pending an ammunition-study assessment. Both the 2018 contract and the 2026 increase put 70% of the work in Oberndorf, Germany.
  • Durability and reliability. Neller said everything he had seen showed the M27s to be "the most reliable, durable, and accurate weapons". But 2016 Army testing found the M855A1 round failed the M27's reliability requirement and might degrade bolt and barrel durability; Marine officials blamed most stoppages on magazines and approved polymer ones. A leaked 2015 report also noted stoppages at high rates of fire in the designated-marksman role later filled by the M38.
  • Firepower. MARSOC kept the M249, arguing its belt-fed volume of fire suits small units better.

FAQ

Is the M27 the same as the HK416?

Almost: a 16.5-inch HK416 with a slightly modified gas block and Marine-specified accessories, under a Marine Corps designation.

What calibre does the M27 fire?

5.56×45mm NATO, from standard 30-round magazines, the same cartridge as the M4 and M249.

Did the M27 replace the M249 SAW?

Largely, in rifle squads, from 2011. Rifle companies kept some M249s and Marine Raiders retained the SAW.

Is the M27 better than the M4?

Marine officials credit it with 100–150 m more range than the M4. It is also heavier and has cost more per rifle.

Will the Marines adopt the Army's M7?

Not for now. In February 2026 the Corps said it would retain the M27 for close-combat units and monitor the M7.
